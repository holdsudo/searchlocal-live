Most people lease by walking into the dealership for the car they want. A lease broker works the other way around: you say what you want, and the broker contacts dealers and manufacturer lease programs for you. Neither route is automatically cheaper. This article explains what actually differs, what does not, and what to ask whichever you choose.

What does not change: the lease itself

A broker does not write the lease. Whether you sign at a showroom or at your kitchen table, the lease is a contract with a lessor, usually the manufacturer's finance arm or a bank, and the same federal rules apply. Under the Consumer Leasing Act and the Federal Reserve's Regulation M (12 CFR Part 1013), the lessor has to put certain figures in writing before you are bound. They include the amount due at signing, itemized; the gross capitalized cost; any capitalized cost reduction; the total of payments; and the terms for early termination and excess wear and mileage.

Those disclosures are your comparison tool. The same car can be quoted with different down payments, terms and mileage limits, so a low monthly payment on one quote tells you little until the other numbers are alongside it.

What is negotiable

The Federal Reserve's consumer leasing guide lists the items you can negotiate: the agreed-upon vehicle value, the capitalized cost reduction, the lease term, the mileage allowance, and optional add-ons such as accessories and maintenance or protection packages. It says the agreed-upon vehicle value is the main part of the gross capitalized cost, and that a lower value means lower monthly payments. It also notes that manufacturer incentives can leave little room to negotiate on some cars, and that the rent charge may be set by a third party and not be negotiable.

The FTC's advice points the same way: shop for a lease the way you would shop for a purchase, negotiate every term including the price of the vehicle, and get every term in writing.

What a broker adds

The practical difference is reach and time. A dealership can offer what is on its lot and the programs its brand offers. A broker is not tied to one lot. According to its Why Us page, Worldwide Auto Leasing works with franchise dealers and manufacturer finance companies for more than 30 brands, so a quote can come from whichever dealer has the car you want, rather than from one store. The same page says the company handles the paperwork, arranges plates, registration and an insurance binder before delivery, and delivers to your home or office in New York, New Jersey and Pennsylvania.

That is the company's description of its own service. We have not tested it against any dealership, and no official source we found ranks brokers against dealers. What you can verify yourself is the quote: ask for the figures listed above in writing and compare them with a dealer's.

How a broker gets paid

This is the first thing to ask any broker, and the answer should be specific. Worldwide's site says it is paid by the dealer on the transaction, the way a dealership's own sales staff is, and that no extra fee is added to your lease unless it tells you in writing before you agree. Other brokers charge the customer a fee instead. Neither arrangement is wrong, but you should know which one you are in before you agree to anything.

Questions to ask, whichever you choose

  • What is the capitalized cost, and what is the vehicle's price before any fees or add-ons?
  • What is the money factor, and is it the rate the lessor set or has it been marked up?
  • What is the residual value, and what mileage limit and term does it assume?
  • What is due at signing, itemized line by line?
  • What are the charges for excess wear, excess mileage and early termination?
  • Are any add-ons included, and are they optional?
  • Who is the lessor, and will the lease be subject to credit approval before the car is delivered?

A broker or dealer who answers these in writing, before you sign, gives you something concrete to compare. If either one is vague about them, that tells you something too.

When a dealership may suit you better

If you want to drive the car first, see the exact vehicle, or sit in a showroom to compare trims, the dealership gives you that directly. Some people also prefer to deal with the store that will service the car. A broker suits people who know the model and would rather compare dealers by phone and have the car delivered. Many people get a broker quote and a dealer quote and compare them line by line.
